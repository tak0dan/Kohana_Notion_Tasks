# Review: *The Art of War* by Sun Tzu

*The Art of War* is a short Chinese military treatise from roughly 2,500 years ago. Its fame comes less from its battlefield advice than from its central idea: the best victory is the one that costs the least. For a geopolitics reader that is the part that still holds, because the book is about strategy under uncertainty, and states are always in that position.

Quotations are from Lionel Giles's translation (chapter.paragraph). The full set of passages is in `art_of_war_all_quotes.md`.

## Winning without fighting

Sun Tzu treats war as a last resort and an expensive one. "There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare" (II.6). He ranks the options by cost: the highest form of generalship is to baulk the enemy's plans (III.3), and the worst is to besiege cities. His conclusion is the book's best-known line: "to fight and conquer in all your battles is not supreme excellence; supreme excellence consists in breaking the enemy's resistance without fighting" (III.2). Modern equivalents are sanctions, alliance-building, deterrence and economic pressure, all of which aim to change an opponent's calculation before force is needed. The "skilful leader subdues the enemy's troops without any fighting; he captures their cities without laying siege to them" (III.6).

## Information and deception

The book puts intelligence first. "If you know the enemy and know yourself, you need not fear the result of a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, for every victory gained you will also suffer a defeat" (III.18). The same logic explains why states invest so heavily in intelligence, and why misjudging an opponent's intentions, or one's own limits, is among the most common causes of failed foreign policy. Sun Tzu is equally blunt about deceit: "All warfare is based on deception" (I.18), and "when able to attack, we must seem unable" (I.19). A strong state should look weak, and a weak one strong, so the opponent misjudges the balance.

## Planning before action

"In war the victorious strategist only seeks battle after the victory has been won, whereas he who is destined to defeat first fights and afterwards looks for victory" (IV.15). Outcomes are largely decided by preparation, such as logistics, alliances, timing and terrain, before any fighting begins. This is a useful lens on decisions to go to war that lacked an exit plan. A related line is a neat statement of why strategy is hard to copy: "All men can see the tactics whereby I conquer, but what none can see is the strategy out of which victory is evolved" (VI.27).

## Adaptability

Sun Tzu rejects fixed doctrine. "Water shapes its course according to the nature of the ground over which it flows; the soldier works out his victory in relation to the foe whom he is facing" (VI.31). Strategy has to respond to the opponent and the terrain, not to a template.

## The rest of the book, briefly

Much of the remaining text is practical or close to truism. Lines such as "Ponder and deliberate before you make a move" (VII.21), "Rapidity is the essence of war" (XI.19) or "Move not unless you see an advantage" (XII.17) are sensible but add little once the ideas above are understood. Large parts of the later chapters are terrain, camp and army-march details that are specific to ancient warfare, and the final chapter on spies is the one other section that stays relevant to intelligence work.

## Limits

The book is compressed and aphoristic. It gives principles, not method, and many lines are close to truisms until applied to a case. It also assumes a unitary ruler who controls the army, which fits poorly with modern democracies, coalitions and public opinion. Read as a source of habits of thought (cost-awareness, information, flexibility) and not as a manual, it remains worth reading.
