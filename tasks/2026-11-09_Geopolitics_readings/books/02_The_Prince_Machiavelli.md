# Review: *The Prince* by Niccolò Machiavelli (written 1513, published 1532)

## Overview
A short handbook of political advice, addressed to a ruler of Renaissance Italy, a fragmented region of rival city-states and foreign invasions. Machiavelli, a former Florentine diplomat, tries to explain how a new ruler can win and keep power.

## Main ideas
- **Politics follows its own logic.** Machiavelli separates politics from conventional morality. A ruler should judge actions by their results for the survival and stability of the state, not by how virtuous they look.
- **Virtù and fortuna.** Success depends on skill and decisiveness (*virtù*) and on luck (*fortuna*). Fortune cannot be controlled, but a prepared ruler can reduce its damage, like building dykes before a flood.
- **Fear versus love.** Ideally a ruler is both loved and feared, but if one must be chosen, fear is more reliable, as long as it does not turn into hatred.
- **Appearances.** A ruler must seem merciful, faithful and honest, but be ready to act otherwise when the state is at stake.
- **Own armies.** Mercenaries and borrowed troops are unreliable, so a state should rely on its own citizens' forces.

## Strengths
Direct, concrete and based on historical examples (Cesare Borgia is the main model). It founded modern political realism, the idea that states act on power and interest.

## Weaknesses
It is aimed at a single ruler and gives little room for institutions, law or public consent. Some advice is cynical, and later readers argue about whether the book is sincere advice, satire, or a plea for Italian unity.

## Relevance to geopolitics
Realist theory, from Hobbes to Morgenthau and Mearsheimer, builds on this view of power and interest. It is useful for understanding why states break promises, form alliances of convenience and put security first. Compared with *The Art of War*, it is more about political legitimacy and the ruler's character, while Sun Tzu focuses on military strategy.
